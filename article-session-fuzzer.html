The Bugs Between Hands: Fuzzing Whole Poker Sessions

By Nine High Studios · October 2026 · 7 min read
Testing Fuzzing Open Source

Our engine reproduces the finishing stacks of 10,000 out of 10,000 real Pluribus hands. Tables still froze. The bugs weren't in any hand. They were in the space between hands, which no hand history ever tests. So we built a fuzzer that plays whole sessions, checked it against rules that must always hold, and open-sourced it as poker-session-fuzzer.

Why hand histories miss it

Hand-history corpora (PHH, site histories, solver output) are wonderful for testing betting, side pots and showdowns. But every hand in them starts from a known state: these players, in these seats, with this button and these blinds. The step that produces that state never runs:

  • who busted and where the button moves next;
  • who's sitting out;
  • whether a player who sat down mid-hand is dealt in;
  • who gets the "deal next hand" button.

That step is where our live table freezes came from.

Other engines don't help much as an oracle, because most of them also model one hand at a time. And multi-hand site histories disagree with each other on dead-button and missed-blind rules, so comparing against them mostly finds rule differences, not bugs.

No oracle needed

Instead of asking "what's the right answer?", the fuzzer asks "is anything impossible true?" It plays random sessions of cash games and sit-and-gos, with 2 to 9 seats and short stacks so players bust often. Players join and leave, sit in and out, time out, and climb the blind levels. Most moves come from each player's own legal actions. After every action it checks invariants:

  • No panic, on any action, legal or not.
  • Status matches stack: a busted player has no chips, and an all-in player has none behind.
  • Chip conservation: chips only appear or vanish when a player joins, tops up or leaves.
  • Liveness: whoever is on the clock can always move the game on.
  • Positions: the big blind is a player in the hand, and the small and big blind are different seats.
  • No stall between hands: if enough players with chips want to play, someone is offered the next hand.

It drives our engine through the exact JSON path our chain uses, rebuilding the game from serialized state on every action. Bugs where the in-memory engine is right but the serialized state loses something have bitten us before, so that matters.

What it found

Its first sweep, 200 sessions in about four seconds, found real bugs straight away. Two had already been fixed in the engine and released to mainnet by the time we wrote this, so we can describe them:

  • An out-of-turn "new hand" reset a hand in progress. The engine had a check for "has the hand finished?", but the dispatcher never called it. A seated player could send a new-hand action mid-hand, and the pot was wiped. On an empty table the same call crashed the engine. The fix was one call to the existing check.
  • Both blinds all-in, and the pot vanished. Late in a heads-up sit-and-go, both blinds can cover both stacks, so nobody is left to act. The engine ended the hand before dealing it, with no winner. The fix had been written the week before on a feature branch and hadn't reached the main line yet. The fuzzer found it again in seconds.

It also found a handful of seating-transition bugs that are still open. All of them are ways a table can stop dealing when players sit down, sit out or leave at particular moments. Each is filed with a reproduction, and we'll write them up once they're fixed. Finally, it showed that a fix we'd just merged for collapsed blind positions was only partial: hits dropped from 46 sessions in 200 to 9, which is a useful kind of feedback to get within minutes.

Keeping CI green without hiding bugs

A fuzzer that fails on every known bug is a fuzzer people turn off. So known bugs are listed by issue number. Their violations are counted and play continues, and only a new kind of violation fails the run. When a fix lands, its entry is removed, and from then on the sweep guards it.

From a random byte stream to a readable test

A fuzz failure is a stream of random bytes, which nobody wants to debug. So every failure prints the session as a script, flags any action the engine accepted that wasn't in the player's legal actions, and renders the session as a scenario in our test language, poker-scene-dsl. The fuzzer's clock and the scenario's clock tick identically, so the scenario replays the session exactly. We checked this on all 200 sweep sessions. The fuzzer then minimises it automatically, replaying smaller and smaller pieces of the session until only the actions that matter are left. One 113-action table stall comes down to 12 sentences, ready to commit as a test.

Try it on your engine

The harness doesn't know anything about our engine. You implement two methods, tell me the table and apply this action, map the vocabulary to your engine's, and add three one-line tests: a sweep for CI, a replay for debugging, and a Go fuzz target for overnight runs. If your engine handles anything between hands (busts, the button, sitting out, late joins), there's a good chance this finds something.
