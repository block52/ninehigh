The Nit, the Station, and the Maniac: What On-Chain Poker Data Says About How You Play

By Nine High Studios · October 2026 · 6 min read
Data Indexer Transparency Poker

Every hand dealt on Block 52 is settled on a public blockchain, and our indexer keeps the receipts. Usually we use that data for serious things — auditing shuffle randomness, proving the deck is fair. Today we used it for something better: finding out who the biggest nit is.

The Dataset

Our public indexer at indexer.block52.xyz watches the chain and records every hand, every action, every buy-in and cash-out. Over the last three weeks it has seen:

2,290 Hands dealt
50 Games
50 Wallets at the tables
21 Days of play

Everything below comes from the indexer's public REST API — the same one anybody can query. No private database, no admin panel. That's the point of the whole system: if we can compute it, so can you.

One note on method. For each wallet the indexer records how its decisions break down — folds, checks, calls, bets and raises. We measured looseness as the share of decisions where a player voluntarily put money in the pot (call, bet or raise), nittiness as the share where they folded, and the classic aggression factor as bets-plus-raises divided by calls. We only ranked wallets with at least 150 recorded decisions, so nobody wins an award for folding three hands and going home.

You could not script a better poker morality tale. The aggressive player who wins more than half the hands he plays is the only one in profit. The calling station — who calls two and a half times more often than he bets or raises — is the biggest loser by a mile. And the nit folded 46% of his decisions, won one single hand, and still lost money. Fifty years of poker literature, confirmed by a Postgres query.

Every one of these numbers is public. The addresses are on-chain identities, the hands are settled on a public blockchain, and the indexer API that produced this table is open to anyone. Your poker stats aren't our data — they're everybody's. Play accordingly.

The Bug We Found on the Way

Here's the part we didn't plan. While pulling these numbers we noticed the table boss's profile endpoint claiming he'd made 24,168 actions, while the player directory — computed by a different code path — said 2,014. A tidy 12× disagreement.

The cause was a classic SQL mistake: the live stats query joined the sessions table to the actions table on player address alone, so every session row got multiplied by the player's total action count — a cross join hiding inside an innocent-looking LEFT JOIN. A player with three sessions and five hundred actions would report five hundred times his real buy-in total.

Because the indexer is open source, the fix is public too: the query now aggregates each table separately and combines the results — the same shape the database's own refresh function already used. The bug was filed, fixed, reviewed and merged the same morning these awards were computed.

Transparency cuts both ways. The same open data that lets us name the loosest player at the table lets anyone catch our bugs. We think that's a fair trade — it's the entire premise of the product.

Caveats, Because We Keep Receipts

  • Playing-style numbers are derived from recorded action counts, not a full VPIP/PFR engine — that's coming to the indexer's aggregate pipeline.
  • Net-profit figures count completed buy-ins and cash-outs; wallets still sitting at a table have buy-ins recorded but no cash-out yet, so individual nets are directional, not final.
  • Three weeks and fifty wallets is a small sample. The nit may yet become a maniac. We'll be watching. So can you.
